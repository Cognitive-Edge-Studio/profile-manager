# How to Hire a Freelance Full-Stack Developer Without a Technical Background

> **At a glance**
>
> This guide explains how to hire a full stack developer when you don't have a technical background. You'll learn how to decide what help you need, assess a freelancer's work, and compare proposals. It also covers scope, costs, milestones, account access, launch, handover, and support, so you know what to agree on before committing to the build.

A website that explains your services is a different job from an application where customers sign in, upload documents, and track requests. Both run in a browser, but they can call for different skills and budgets.

Before you hire a full-stack developer, get clear on what people need to do with the software. Then look for relevant work, ask how the developer would approach your project, and agree on a first stage you can review.

You can take part in those decisions without reading code. Here's how I'd work through them.

## Decide whether you need a full-stack developer

If you need a business website with service pages, articles, and a contact form, someone experienced with WordPress or a website builder may be a suitable choice. Ask how you will update the content and what maintenance it will need. Custom development should have a reason you can understand.

A full-stack developer works on the screens people use, often called the frontend, and the logic and data behind them, called the backend. For a customer portal, that could mean building the request form, saving each request, and making sure customers can see only their own information.

That range can suit a small custom application or a feature that touches several parts of an existing product. If your software already uses a particular technology, look for someone who can work with it and assess it before suggesting major changes.

The title doesn't tell you whether the person also handles visual design, writing, testing, hosting, or ongoing support. Ask which responsibilities their proposal includes.

One freelancer also has limited capacity. A tight deadline with several streams of work, specialist security requirements, or a service needing support around the clock may call for a team or additional specialists. Ask what the developer can deliver alone and where they would need help.

## Write a short project brief before asking for quotes

Start with the problem, the people affected, and the task they need to complete. You don't need to choose a programming language before you can explain the work.

Describe what happens today and what should happen after the project. Include the first version's essential features, what can wait, and any existing software it must connect to. Share your budget range and preferred deadline, including why that date matters.

Here is an illustrative brief for an imaginary customer portal:

> Customers currently email us to ask about their service requests. We want them to sign in and see the latest status themselves. Staff need to update that status and leave a note the customer can read.
>
> The first version needs customer accounts, a request list, a request detail page, and staff updates. Customers must see only their own requests. Payments, live chat, and a mobile app can wait.
>
> We have rough screen designs and an existing spreadsheet of requests. We need advice on whether importing that spreadsheet belongs in the first stage. Before requesting a build estimate, we will share our available budget and the date we hope to begin a customer pilot.

For your own brief, add links to designs or a prototype if you have them. Explain what those screens should do. A clickable design may show a successful sign-in without covering forgotten passwords, failed attempts, or different account permissions.

If you are improving existing software, share what works, what is broken, and what access is available for an assessment. An estimate made before inspecting unfamiliar code needs to state that limitation.

Send the same brief to each shortlisted developer. Their questions will help you improve it, and comparable information will make their proposals easier to judge.

## Where to find a freelance full-stack developer

Ask business owners or people in your professional network who built software similar to what you need. Find out what that person actually delivered and how the collaboration went.

You can also look through independent developer websites, professional networks, communities, and freelance marketplaces. Use these places to find candidates, then assess each person. A referral, profile rating, or persuasive reply gives you a starting point.

Look for work with similar responsibilities. A developer who has handled customer permissions and document access may be relevant to a portal, even if the previous application served another industry.

When you reach out, send your brief and ask for one or two relevant examples. Confirm who will do the work. If someone else will contribute or take over communication, you should know before agreeing to the project.

## Check what the developer actually did on past projects

A portfolio can show a finished product without showing the person's contribution. Ask them to walk you through a relevant piece of work.

Which parts did they build? Was it personal work, employment, or a freelance engagement? Did they start from scratch or join an existing team? Who handled design, testing, and launch?

Then ask them to explain one decision. For example, why did they use an existing service for payments, or how did they decide which features to leave out of the first version? Listen for the requirements and tradeoffs behind the choice.

Where possible, ask for a demonstration of a complete task. For a portal, that might be submitting a request and finding it again after signing back in. A demonstration helps you understand the work, although it cannot establish everything about the code underneath.

Some projects are private or covered by confidentiality agreements. A developer may be able to give an anonymized explanation, show permitted material, or demonstrate a personal project instead. Respect those boundaries.

For a substantial engagement, ask whether a past client or colleague is willing to speak with you. Useful reference questions concern the person's actual responsibilities, how they handled changes, and whether the handover was usable. A reference is another piece of evidence, rather than a guarantee.

## Ask questions you can assess without reading code

Bring the conversation back to your project. You should leave with a clearer understanding of the next step, the uncertainties, and your responsibilities.

| Question to ask | What a useful answer should explain |
| --- | --- |
| What would you need to understand before estimating this? | The missing information, any access needed, and which unknowns could change the estimate. |
| What would you include in the first version, and what could wait? | A usable customer task, with a reason for including or postponing each major feature. |
| How would you check that the important parts work? | Concrete checks, such as a customer being unable to view another customer's requests, plus what you will review yourself. |
| How will I see progress? | When you will get a working demonstration or test link, what you can try, and how feedback will be recorded. |
| What happens if I change a requirement? | How the change is assessed and approved, including its effect on price and timing. |
| What will I need to run this after launch? | The required accounts, recurring services, handover material, and separately agreed support. |

You can also ask why the developer recommends particular tools and what those choices mean for future maintenance. Request an explanation in terms of your project if the answer becomes a list of unfamiliar names.

Pay attention to how they handle uncertainty. A useful answer can identify something that needs investigation and explain how to investigate it. Be cautious about a firm promise that leaves important questions unanswered, or repeated difficulty explaining what is included.

For a project with sensitive data, complex integrations, or a large commitment, consider a paid independent technical review of the proposed approach or assessment work. Your conversations help you judge clarity and fit. A qualified reviewer can examine technical details you cannot assess yourself.

## Use a small paid assessment when important questions remain

Sometimes the sensible first purchase is an answer to a specific question. You might need to know whether existing code can support a new feature or whether another service can provide the data your application needs.

Agree on the assessment's question, price or spending limit, deliverable, and stopping point before it begins. Depending on the uncertainty, the result could be a written review, a revised scope, or a small working demonstration.

For the imaginary portal, an assessment might examine a sample of the spreadsheet and test how requests can be matched to the right customer accounts. The agreed output could include a small import demonstration using fictional records, a list of missing or inconsistent fields, and a recommendation on whether to include the import in the first stage.

That would be a limited feasibility check. A complete, customer-ready portal would need its own scope, testing, and launch work.

Pay for the agreed work and review both the result and the collaboration. Did the developer explain the findings, stay within the agreed boundary, and identify the remaining questions? Use that evidence to decide whether to continue. A paid assessment does not have to commit either person to the full build.

## How much does it cost to hire a full-stack developer?

The cost to hire a freelance full-stack developer depends on the work they are taking responsibility for. Screen count alone tells you little about permissions, business rules, integrations, existing code, or testing.

Design work, moving old data, supporting different devices, preparing the live system, and review cycles can all affect an estimate. Ask which assumptions the price depends on and what could change it.

To give you a starting point, here are hourly figures published by freelance marketplaces and a development-company directory. All amounts are in US dollars and were checked on 24 October 2026.

| Where you hire | Published hourly rate | What to check before you commit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Upwork | $16 to $35 per hour | Upwork reports this range for certain past contracts worldwide. Ask the developer to estimate the hours your own project needs. |
| Fiverr | $25 to $200 per hour | Fiverr's guide gives this broad range. Before ordering, check what the seller will build and what costs extra. |
| Arc | $61 to $80 per hour | Arc displays this as its average band for full-stack developers worldwide, across experience levels. Ask for a quote based on your requirements. |
| Toptal | Request a quote | Toptal asks you to discuss your project and hiring requirements to understand the price. Confirm the scope and level of experience included. |
| Development company, using Clutch's data | $25 to $49 per hour | Clutch reports this as its average company range across locations. Ask which team members are included and how their time will be billed. |

Use these numbers to help you ask questions about a quote. They cover different groups of developers and companies, so they do not tell you which option will cost less for your project. Compare the estimated hours, deliverables, and responsibilities. Platform fees, applicable taxes, and running costs may add to the total.

If you are looking at a small fixed-price package, check whether it covers a complete build, a single feature, or just one stage of the work.

You may see these terms in a proposal. Check how each applies to your project:

- With a **fixed price**, you agree on a price for a defined scope. Check the exclusions and change process carefully. If a major unknown remains, it may need an assessment before the developer can offer a useful fixed quote.

- With **hourly work**, you pay for time spent. Ask for an estimate, understandable work records, and a spending limit or approval point. Agree when the developer must raise a possible overrun so you can make a decision before more cost is incurred.

- **Milestones** describe stages of delivery. Either pricing model can use them. They should make the work reviewable, with clear payment and approval arrangements.

When comparing proposals, put the scope side by side. Does each include design, testing, launch, and handover? Are revisions defined? What information or materials must you supply? What happens if access to an outside service is delayed?

A lower quote might cover a smaller job. A higher one might include work you don't need yet. Ask each developer to explain the difference before deciding on price.

Request a separate list of running costs. Depending on the project, these may include the domain, hosting, database, storage, email, paid software licences, and charges for connected services. Ask what is fixed, what grows with usage, and who pays each bill. Maintenance and future features also need a budget if you want them.

I would choose the proposal whose scope and assumptions I can understand, then check whether that commitment fits the available budget.

## Agree on milestones and scope changes in writing

A useful milestone describes something you can check. For the illustrative portal, one stage could let a customer sign in, see their own requests, and open a request's details on a private test site.

The acceptance criteria are the checks you both agree show that stage is complete. In this example, they would include keeping another customer's requests inaccessible. Ask the developer to help turn broad feature names into clear behavior, including important failure cases.

Put the delivery arrangements in writing so you can refer back to them when reviewing work or requesting a change:

- **Stage details and payments.** For each stage, record the deliverable, target date, review period, payment arrangement, and dependencies. Agree how reported defects will be handled and how an addition will be distinguished from a correction to agreed work. Record any deposit and later payment triggers so both sides know what happens next.

- **Scope changes.** When you request a change, the developer should explain its effect on the plan. You can then approve the added cost, exchange it for something else, or leave it for later. Capture that decision before the new work starts.

- **Other agreement terms.** The agreement should also cover confidentiality, ownership and permitted use of the work, third-party licences, access, and what will be handed over if the project ends early. Make these terms explicit between the parties.

## Make progress easy to review

Before work starts, choose a place for project decisions and agree on update and review times. For remote work, confirm time zones, useful overlap, and expectations for replies.

A short update should explain what changed, what you can review, what is blocked, and which decision is needed from you. At review points, try the software in a private test environment, often called staging, before changes reach customers.

Follow a real task from beginning to end. Try submitting incomplete information or using an account with different permissions where that is relevant. Ask which checks the developer has already completed and which issues remain. Screenshots can show appearance, but a working test link lets you examine behavior.

Your part matters too. Provide the agreed content and access, combine feedback from your team, and make decisions within the review period. If progress slips, ask what caused the delay and request an updated plan with the next reviewable result.

## Plan account access, launch, and handover before starting

Agree on these arrangements before development begins. They explain how you will access the work, put it into use, and look after it once the build is complete.

- **Account access.** Ask which accounts the project needs and arrange business control of the domain, hosting, source code, and other essential services where possible. Source code should be kept in a repository, a shared home for the code and its change history, with access agreed from the beginning.

    Use individual invitations and appropriate permissions when services support them. Keep account recovery details under your business's control. The developer can help set this up and explain any service restrictions that affect access or transfer.

- **Launch.** Agree who handles deployment, which means putting the software into the live environment. The plan should include final checks, any data transfer, backups, and how to recover if the release fails. Decide who approves launch and who checks the important tasks afterward.

- **Handover.** Define the handover as a deliverable. Depending on the project, it should include the agreed source code, setup and deployment instructions, account and service inventory, backup and restore instructions, known limitations, and guidance for routine administration. Agree whether training or a walkthrough is included.

    Check that you can access what was promised. If another developer will take over, allow time for them to review the documentation and ask questions.

- **Ongoing support.** Support after launch needs a separate agreement. Clarify any included defect-fixing period, ongoing maintenance, response expectations, fees, and how new work is requested. A handover gives you information and access. Continued availability needs to be discussed and arranged.

## A short checklist before you hire

Before committing to the main build, check that you can answer these questions:

- Does this project fit one developer's skills and capacity?
- Have we agreed on the first version and what can wait?
- Do I understand their role in the relevant work they showed me?
- Have we resolved the major unknowns or agreed on a paid assessment?
- Can I explain the price, assumptions, and separate running costs?
- Are milestones, reviews, payments, and scope changes documented?
- Are account access, ownership terms, launch, and handover agreed?
- Do I know what support is included and what needs a separate arrangement?
