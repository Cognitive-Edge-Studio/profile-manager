# Prototype vs MVP: What Should You Build Before Paying for a Full Product?

> **At a glance**
>
> This Prototype vs MVP guide helps you choose what to build before committing to a larger software project. You'll learn what each approach tests, when a technical check or simpler experiment may be enough, and what can carry forward. It also explains what affects the work and what to agree with a developer before paying for the next step.

The prototype vs MVP question comes down to what you need to learn before paying for software development. You might need to see whether people understand a booking process. You might need to check whether your supplier's system will share the data your idea depends on. Or you might need customers to use a small service in their normal work.

Those questions need different tests.

A prototype helps you test a proposed experience. A minimum viable product, or MVP, helps you learn whether an important customer or business assumption holds up. Sometimes a conversation, a manual service, or a focused technical test is the right place to start.

## What is the difference between a prototype and an MVP?

A prototype makes a proposed solution tangible so people can explore and test it. It might be a paper sketch, a set of clickable screens, or something built with code.

For example, someone could try choosing an appointment and changing its date in a prototype. The screens might respond convincingly even though no appointment is saved.

The level of detail should suit the question. In its [guidance on prototype fidelity](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-prototype-hi-lo-fidelity/), Nielsen Norman Group describes prototypes that vary in both realism and interactivity, with testing based on watching people attempt tasks.

An MVP serves a learning goal. In his [explanation of an MVP](https://leanstartup.co/resources/articles/what-is-an-mvp/), Eric Ries describes learning about customers with the least effort and makes clear that the right form depends on the context.

That is why people use the term differently. In Lean discussions, an MVP can be a small experiment. In a development proposal, it often means an initial usable product. Ask what the person means before comparing quotes.

For a usable software MVP, people need to complete the task you have promised. Some work behind the scenes may still be manual. A form connected to a service you operate yourself could be enough to test its value. An elaborate automated system may be unnecessary at that point.

These labels can overlap. A prototype used in a customer experiment may contribute to an MVP approach. The useful distinction is what you are testing and what the result will let you decide.

## Prototype vs MVP at a glance

The MVP column below describes a small version that delivers a real outcome to its users. Compare it with a prototype by the question each can answer, the feedback you can collect, and the work that continues afterwards.

| Decision | Prototype | Usable MVP |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main question | Can people understand and use this proposed experience? | Will the intended users get value from this version in practice? |
| Who takes part | Intended users, with the founder, designer, or developer observing | Early customers or internal users, plus whoever runs and supports the service |
| What needs to work | Enough of the experience to test the chosen task. Other behavior can be simulated | The promised task must reach a real outcome. Supporting work may be manual |
| Useful feedback | Where people hesitate, misunderstand, or need help | Whether people finish the task, return, pay where relevant, and need support |
| What may carry forward | Findings, content, designs, and code that is suitable for reuse | A working service that may be improved, changed, or replaced |
| Continuing work | Arrange testing, record findings, and revise the idea | Handle the agreed service, user issues, data, fixes, and operating costs |
| Limits of this evidence | A usability session does not establish demand, technical feasibility, or readiness for live use | Early use does not establish long-term demand, profitable operation, or suitability for a much larger audience |

## MVP vs prototype: which should you build first?

Choose the next step by the question you need to answer. Think about what would change your decision to invest further, then choose a test that can provide that evidence. The questions below help you identify a useful starting point.

### Is the problem worth solving?

Start with people who experience the problem. Ask how they handle it now, what goes wrong, and what they have already tried. Then consider a specific offer they can respond to.

A simple page inviting people to request a trial may help you test interest. A manual service may let you test whether the outcome is useful before you automate delivery. These are different experiments within the broader [MVP learning process described by Yevgeniy Brikman](https://www.ybrikman.com/blog/2025/02/10/mvp-is-a-process/).

Keep the conclusion narrow. Someone leaving an email address has agreed to hear more. You still need evidence about use and payment.

### Can people use the proposed solution?

A prototype is useful when the uncertainty concerns the experience. Choose the task you want to observe, such as submitting a request or comparing options. This lets you examine the flow before connecting the systems behind it.

Give intended users a realistic situation and let them try. Record where they need help and what they expect to happen. If you explain every screen as they go, you are learning how well they follow your demonstration.

Before testing, decide which behavior must be realistic and which parts can be left out.

### Can the technology do the essential job?

Ask for a technical proof of concept when an untested capability could prevent the service from working. This is a focused feasibility test. Agree on the capability to test and the evidence that would show it works.

For example, can your application retrieve the required records from a supplier's system? You may need a small working connection and a record of its limits before you need a designed customer portal.

The UK government's Government Digital Service (GDS) gives integration feasibility as an example in its [guidance on testing risky assumptions](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/agile-delivery/how-the-alpha-phase-works). The principle is useful here even though the guidance concerns UK public services.

A successful test answers the agreed technical question. It leaves other questions, such as usability and demand, open.

### Will people use the service in practice?

Choose a usable MVP when you need people to complete a real task to learn anything further. Let them finish the promised job in their usual setting, while you observe the result and the help they need.

Define the intended users, the outcome, and what you will observe. Depending on the service, useful evidence might include completed tasks, repeat use, payment, or the amount of help required.

Agree when you will review that evidence and what could make you continue, change direction, or stop. The Lean Startup's [build-measure-learn approach](https://theleanstartup.com/principles) connects building to that next decision.

You do not have to buy a separate prototype project first if the experience is already understood and a usable version is the appropriate test.

## Three situations that make the choice clearer

The following situations are illustrative. They are not accounts of client projects. Each shows how the main unknown changes what is worth building, what evidence you can collect, and which questions will still need a different test.

- **You are planning an unfamiliar booking process:** The main question is whether customers can choose the right service and change an appointment. A prototype could let you observe those tasks before connecting calendars or taking payments. Watch for misunderstandings and needed explanations. Successful clicks would tell you about the flow, while demand would still need testing.

- **Your customer portal depends on a supplier's stock data:** First establish whether you can access the required records with the available permissions. A technical test can check the connection, the data you receive, and important gaps. One successful request would still leave questions about updates, failures, and day-to-day operation.

- **You want to sell a reporting service to small businesses:** You could prepare an initial report manually and see whether it helps with a real decision. Agree what is being offered and whether it is paid. Observe whether customers request another report and how much work delivery takes. If the next question requires customers to use a service themselves, a small software MVP may be appropriate. A useful manual report would still leave the cost and feasibility of automation to investigate.

The next step follows the uncertainty. It does not have to follow the same sequence in every project.

## Can the prototype become the MVP?

Some of the work may carry forward. Useful findings, clearer wording, tested layouts, and agreed workflows can all inform development. That does not mean the underlying code is ready for customers.

Code needs a separate assessment. A convincing prototype might use sample data or skip access controls, error handling, and other work needed for live use. In its [guidance on coded prototypes](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/design/making-prototypes), GDS explicitly cautions against copying prototype code straight into production.

Ask the developer to explain what can be kept, what needs changing, and why. A demonstration alone does not tell you how much development remains.

The same judgment applies after an MVP. If the initial version serves its users and is suitable to maintain, it can evolve. If the evidence points to a different approach, some work may need replacing. Calling something an MVP does not decide that question in advance.

## What changes the time and investment?

Ask for an estimate against a defined test. The label alone tells you very little about the work. Before comparing proposals, check the scope of the experiment, the work needed to run it, and the responsibilities that continue afterwards.

- **Scope and research:** A prototype covering one decision is a different assignment from a realistic simulation of several user journeys. Research also takes effort. Someone needs to find suitable participants, prepare tasks, observe sessions, and interpret the findings.

- **Connections and technical unknowns:** For a usable service, discuss the systems it connects to, the data it handles, the people who can access it, and the help users may need. Unfamiliar technology and unclear requirements can create additional investigation and revisions.

- **Work after delivery:** Hosting, outside services, manual operations, support, and further changes may continue for as long as the service runs.

Compare proposals using their scope, assumptions, and deliverables. Universal price ranges or fixed build times cannot tell you what your particular test requires.

## What to agree before you pay for the next step

A short written agreement should make these points clear. Use them to make sure you and the developer expect the same result, understand the limits of the work, and know how you will decide what happens next.

- **The question:** What do you need to learn before spending further?
- **The participants:** Who will test or use it, and how will you reach them?
- **The deliverable:** Will you receive screens, working code, a technical result, or a usable service?
- **The limits:** What is real, what is simulated, and what is outside the scope?
- **The evidence:** What will you observe, and how will you record it?
- **The next decision:** When will you review the result, and what would justify continuing, changing, or stopping?
- **The handover:** What will you own or have access to, what may be reused, and who handles ongoing work and costs?

If you want help choosing that first step, bring the problem, what you already know, and the question that would change your decision. That gives a developer something concrete to assess before you commit to a larger build.
